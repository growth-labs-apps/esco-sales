● The Sales Profile · Buying structure and reachability

What it takes to win them.

Three components. Practice type and affiliation describe the buying structure — stable. Time describes reachability — dynamic. The first two determine how the deal gets approved. The third determines when it can happen.

● Buying structure · stable

Practice type

Whether the practice operates as a single practice or multiple practices. Affects how many independent points of authority need to align on the decision. Stable structural feature — doesn't change much over time unless ownership changes.

● Reachability · dynamic

Time.

Where the prospect sits on willingness to change. The only dynamic profile attribute — it can shift on a quality failure, a pricing dispute, or a leadership change at the incumbent vendor.

Unlike practice type and affiliation, time is dynamic. It can shift quickly based on events — a quality failure, a pricing dispute, a leadership change at the incumbent vendor. Same prospect, same structure, but reachability changes.
01
Critical
Actively replacing.

A quality failure, pricing dispute, or change event has broken the incumbent relationship. The prospect is moving — the question is who they move to.

02
Semi-critical
Open at renewal.

Not in distress, but willing to entertain alternatives at the natural decision point. Position yourself before renewal — not after auto-renewal triggers.

03
Non-critical
Defaulting to status quo.

Nothing prompting a change. Reachability is low — long-cycle nurture and waiting for the next reachable moment.

Administrative

Direct decision-making power over vendor selection, performance management, and the facility services budget. Owns the day-to-day relationship with the cleaning vendor. As an organization grows in size, locations, and proximity, this authority can spread across multiple roles or sit higher in the organization.

Consequence
Continuation · Escalation
─ Continuation what staff continue to bear →

Time spent on follow-ups, complaints, corrective conversations, and documentation for an underperforming vendor; their professional judgment in selecting and managing the vendor is questioned.

▲ Escalation what it builds toward →

Absorbing costs that flow from consequences at the clinical support and clinical layers, from managing low morale to navigating a serious liability exposure.

At scale

Scale and Administrative Authority

Administrative only

Cross-Site Variation

Different crews, site managers, and possibly vendors, all working at different operational standards increase variation across sites. Identifying whether a cleaning failure is local to a site, common across a vendor, or a pattern across the organization becomes an additional challenge for an administrative authority.

Dispersed Authority

Authority itself can also be dispersed across a wider and deeper administrative structure, adding additional cost and complexity to identifying and resolving clinical-support and clinical issues.

Consolidation

When cleaning is split across multiple vendors, the organization manages multiple separate vendor relationships rather than one. Consolidation could reduce the administrative work of maintaining these relationships and could produce better pricing.

Perceived Quality of Care

When cleanliness slips, patients form a lower impression of the clinical care the practice provides.

Loss of Patient Trust

Visible cleanliness issues erode patient trust in the practice over time.

Reputation Damage

Visible cleanliness issues generate complaints, negative reviews, and word-of-mouth that damage the practice's reputation.

Staff Burden

When cleaning services fail, clinical and administrative staff absorb the consequences through complaints, scope creep, and the friction of working in a poorly maintained space.

Increased Infection Risk

When cleaning services in a healthcare facility fail, the risk of infection transmission increases.
